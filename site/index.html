SHADING

The pale field is the reference level, cool blues to warm reds, interpolated between the listed stations. It is derived here; the official NWS field is the NDFD grid.

REFERENCE

On the day-ahead views it is the NWS forecast. On the current-day views it is what the day is now expected to reach, which is what the station has already recorded taken with the NWS forecast for the hours left. A forecast starts at the current hour, so once the morning has set the day’s low the forecast alone no longer holds it.

LABELS

Philadelphia 88° (−5) means 88°, five degrees below the reference. Labels move out of each other’s way and never cover another station’s dot, so where two cities sit close together one of them may go unlabelled. Its dot is still there and still carries its numbers on hover.

INTERNATIONAL STATIONS AND HONOLULU

These stations settle in Celsius, so their labels carry the unit. They take the same encoding as the map above; a grey dot means the view has no value for that station, because US government feeds carry observations everywhere but forecasts only for the United States and, through the Blend of Models, Canada. Each station is named with its observed reading so far and, where the exchange has a listed board for it, the market’s implied median for the day being shown. With no government forecast to compare against, those two numbers are the whole picture abroad. Click a station for its chart.

Weather prediction markets on this board

A weather prediction market is a contract that pays one dollar if a stated weather outcome happens and nothing if it does not, so its price in cents is the market’s probability of that outcome. The contracts behind this board ask whether the daily high, or the daily low, at one named weather station will finish above or below a stated whole-degree threshold. ForecastEx lists them at 25 stations across the United States and at 12 more abroad, and each settles on that station’s own record of the day.

Every dot is one of those stations. Its colour and size come from the market-implied median for the day, the temperature the listed strikes price as an even chance, set against the National Weather Service forecast for the same station and day. Two public statements about the same afternoon sit beside each other, one from a forecast office and one from people with money on the answer, and the board shows where they stand furthest apart. Clicking a dot opens that station’s own page, with its forecasts, its observations and the ladder of strikes listed on it.

How a temperature contract settles

Settlement runs on the station’s METAR observations, the routine and special reports it publishes through the day, over the local calendar day from midnight to midnight. The highest reading settles the high contracts and the lowest settles the low contracts, in whole degrees Fahrenheit at the United States stations and whole degrees Celsius abroad. A high contract pays Yes only when the settled value is strictly above its threshold, and a low contract only when it is strictly below, so a day landing exactly on the threshold pays No. Worked examples are on the daily temperature markets page.

Prices on these pages

This exchange has no sellers. A participant buys Yes or buys No, the two bids sum to one dollar, and the price shown here is the midpoint between them in cents, as published at the time stamped on the page and never adjusted for fees. Day by day scoring of the market against each forecast tool is on the accuracy page. The records behind the longer-dated contracts are on the rain, drought and severe weather, climate, hurricane, energy and crop yield pages.
