Weather prediction market accuracy

ForecastEx runs daily prediction markets on the high and low temperature in cities across the US. This page scores those markets against eleven public forecasts, the National Weather Service point forecast, the National Blend of Models, the Aviation Forecast, and models from the US, Europe, Germany, Canada, the UK, France, and Japan. Every comparison holds the city, the day, and the lead time fixed, and scores each system against the temperature the station recorded.

LEAD CURVE

Average error of the market and each forecast tool against the recorded temperature, plotted against hours remaining before the day ends. Bands show bootstrap uncertainty over the matched sample.

CITY MAP

Compares market error to each tool’s error at each of the 24 cities, over the time window a reader would actually check, morning of, evening before, or newsletter hours, with the difference and its uncertainty interval.

SCORECARD GRID

Summarizes each system’s error, bias, and share of forecasts within one degree at four leads, scored against both the official settlement and the National Weather Service’s climate report, with each system’s percent difference from the National Weather Service shown alongside.

CALIBRATION AND BRIER SCORE

Checks whether a Yes price of, say, 70 cents paid out about 70 percent of the time, and scores that reliability with the Brier score, split out by lead and by which side of the book is used.

MARKET MOVEMENT BETWEEN FORECAST CYCLES

Tracks how much and how often the market’s price moves between other tools’ forecast updates, how quickly it settles on the final temperature, and how it reacts when a new report moves the observed extreme. One city-day is traced hour by hour as an example.

HOW TO READ THIS PAGE
  • Clock. Lead counts down to station-local midnight, the moment the target day ends. A tool’s lead is timed to when its forecast was captured, the market’s lead is timed to the ladder snapshot itself.
  • Truth. Every system is scored against the station’s METAR settle, the day’s highest or lowest hourly reading, rounded to the nearest whole degree. A toggle rescores tools against the National Weather Service’s climate report instead, a different definition of the day’s extreme that runs about a degree warmer on highs.
  • Market value. A contract’s Yes price is the midpoint between the Yes bid and one dollar minus the No bid, p = (Yes bid + (1 - No bid)) / 2 when both sides are quoted. The ladder across strikes is forced monotone by pooling any violations, and the market’s temperature is where it crosses fifty cents, rounded up for highs and down for lows to match whole-degree settlement.
  • Tool value. A tool’s value at any moment is its most recent forecast at or before that moment for the target date. By default that value is held at the running observed extreme, so a forecast high is never shown below a high that already occurred, and a forecast low is never shown above a low that already occurred.
  • Sample. The matched cohort at a given lead includes only city-days where all eleven tools have a value and the market has a ladder crossing. Thin order books, short capture windows, backfilled rows, and days with gaps in the observation record are excluded and counted, a bin needs at least 30 city-days before it’s drawn.
  • Uncertainty. Every interval on this page is a 95 percent bootstrap over target dates, 1,000 resamples, fixed seed. When a figure shows the market minus a tool, both sides are resampled from the same draws, so the difference reflects paired variation, not independent noise in each side.
