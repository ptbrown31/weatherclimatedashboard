Allocation calculator

A teaching tool for spreading a position across a strike ladder. Type the value you expect and how far off you could be, and it turns that into a probability curve, prices every strike’s Yes and No against it, and sizes a set of purchases three ways — showing where each dollar goes and what the whole set would pay back at every possible outcome. It opens on a made-up example; the picker loads any live ladder on this site. Everything here is arithmetic on the numbers you type and the exchange’s public prices — the site adds no forecast of its own, and none of this is investment advice.

How the sizing works

Your two numbers define a normal curve: the value you expect is its centre, and the 95 percent band is two standard deviations either side. For every strike the calculator compares your probability that the contract pays with what it costs to buy now — Yes at one dollar less the No bid, No at one dollar less the Yes bid, plus the per-side fee — and only a side priced below your probability is a candidate. Sizing uses the Kelly criterion (Kelly, 1956): the aggressive scenario is the allocation that maximises the expected logarithm of wealth under your curve, computed jointly across the whole ladder so overlapping strikes are sized together rather than one at a time. The middle and conservative scenarios stake half and a quarter of that allocation and keep the rest in cash — fractional Kelly, the standard way to give up some expected growth for much smaller drawdowns (MacLean, Thorp & Ziemba, Good and bad properties of the Kelly criterion). On quantities settled in whole units — daily temperatures, storm counts — “above 84” is read as the settled figure rounding past it, so the working threshold sits half a unit beyond the strike, matching how the contracts settle. Dollar figures are turned into whole contracts by rounding down, and whatever that leaves over stays in cash.

What this is not: a forecast, a recommendation, or a fair value. The probabilities are yours — the page only does the arithmetic that follows from them, and if your numbers are wrong the expected values shown are wrong with them. The market’s prices are themselves a probability curve, and when yours and the market’s agree, the calculator correctly buys nothing.
