Allocation calculator

A teaching tool for spreading a position across a strike ladder. Type the value you expect and how far off you could be, and it turns that into a probability curve, prices every strike’s Yes and No against it, and splits the whole amount across the ladder three ways — the entire sum always goes in, and the scenarios differ only in where it goes. Conservative hedges widely and pays back smoothly almost wherever the value lands; aggressive concentrates near your value and pays big only if you are close. The chart shows the prices as they stand, where each dollar goes with its payout multiple, and what the whole set pays at every possible outcome. It opens on a made-up example; the picker loads any live ladder on this site. Everything here is arithmetic on the numbers you type and the exchange’s public prices — the site adds no forecast of its own, and none of this is investment advice.

How the split works

Your two numbers define a normal curve: the value you expect is its centre, and the 95 percent band is two standard deviations either side — that curve says what you think, and the scenario says how much it hurts to be wrong; they are deliberately separate questions. The whole amount is always spent, so the calculator’s only decision is the split: it chooses the one that maximises the expected utility of the payout under your curve, computed jointly across the ladder on the exact intervals between strike thresholds. The middle scenario uses the logarithm as the utility, which is the Kelly criterion (Kelly, 1956) in its original fully-invested form — and it has Kelly’s famous property that when the prices already match your curve, the best split simply mirrors your own probabilities. The conservative and aggressive scenarios replace the logarithm with a more and a less risk-averse member of the same standard power-utility family — the fully-invested equivalents of quarter-Kelly and double-Kelly, the correspondence set out in MacLean, Thorp & Ziemba, Good and bad properties of the Kelly criterion. A more risk-averse utility cannot tolerate outcomes that pay nothing, so it buys the cheap contracts on both flanks and the payout curve comes out smooth; a less risk-averse one accepts them, piles onto the best-priced strikes near your value, and pays several times the money — but only there. Costs are what a buyer pays now: Yes at one dollar less the No bid, No at one dollar less the Yes bid, the per-side fee inside every figure. On quantities settled in whole units — daily temperatures, storm counts — “above 84” is read as the settled figure rounding past it, so the working threshold sits half a unit beyond the strike, matching how the contracts settle. Dollar shares become whole contracts by rounding down, and the loose change then buys one contract at a time, best first, until it cannot afford the cheapest thing left.

What this is not: a forecast, a recommendation, or a fair value. The probabilities are yours — the page only does the arithmetic that follows from them, and if your numbers are wrong the expected values shown are wrong with them. The market’s prices are themselves a probability curve, and when yours and the market’s agree, no split has an edge over another — the differences between the scenarios are then purely about how smooth a payout you want.
